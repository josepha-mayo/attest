Attest

Tamper-evident visit records for home services, built on the Ring Partner API — what the devices observed, what the worker reported, what a human concluded. Records, not verdicts — the record gets a verdict; nobody's story does.

New York's comptroller found $14.5B paid against 82M personal-care visits with no matching record; Ohio's auditor found 34% of electronic-visit-verification entries manually adjusted — invisibly. Editable records are the problem; Attest makes them tamper-evident.

"He said / she said" disputes stop being memory contests: every observation, statement, and conclusion is a separate signed receipt on a hash-chained ledger — disagreements stay on the record, flagged and verbatim, because the record never adjudicates. And the exported pack carries its own verifier: the same verify.html bundled inside the zip is the page this site hosts — it checks any pack you drop on it, ours or a stranger's email attachment.

The trust chain — nobody in it is trusted


"The record gets a verdict;
nobody's story does."


Every observation and statement becomes a signed receipt; receipts chain by
hash into a publicly checkable Merkle tree; an independent witness
cosigns the tree head. Export a pack and strangers verify it — in a browser,
in bare Python, in an AWS Lambda — without asking us, Ring, or the issuer
whether the bytes are still honest.

Ring's event history is Ring's word. An Attest receipt verifies
without Ring — and without us. Incumbent tracking puts a GPS ankle monitor on
the worker's phone, spoofable and resented; Attest anchors observation to the
household's own premises, with the household's own cameras. And the trend cuts
our way: as Ring moves traffic toward end-to-end encryption (TAKE), the
platform holds less and less producible content — which makes a
signed, independently checkable evidence trail more valuable, not
less. Encryption decides who can see the bytes; attestation decides whether
anyone can quietly change them.

Mint your own: attest export --tamper-twin twin.zip --attack
missing-record — the exporter self-verifies the twin and refuses to ship
it if the doctored copy still passes.



Or skip the shipped attacks entirely: the tamper lab lets you
commit the forgery yourself — pick a signed field in the sample pack, type
your own number, and the verifier rebuilds a real zip around your edit,
downloads it for you, and refuses it. Nothing is staged: the doctored pack
is a real artifact, and the same signature catches your number,
not just ours.



And the harder corpus, public: 36
conformance vectors — the Wycheproof-style packs every verifier
surface must judge identically. The interesting attacks aren't obvious tampers;
they're grammar traps — leaf_index: true, "5" as a
string, 5.5 fractional, space-padded hex, a tree_size of
240 that breaks JS bitwise ops. Each row is a one-click
verify.html deep link landing on the verdict the oracle predicts.
Two verifiers silently diverging is exactly how a pack gets ruled valid in one
room and invalid in another — this corpus exists so ours can't.



The fork nobody can hide


The transparency log's whole point: even we can't rewrite history. One
issuer key, two histories — a witness pins one tree head, a monitor pins the
other. Neither side can tell alone; together they prove the fork, and the catch
itself is signed as a monitor_observation receipt committed inside a
signed tree head. Verify the caught
fork in your browser — the evidence doc's checkpoint signature, inclusion
proofs, and embedded observation all check out. Reproduce it:
attest split-demo fabricates the whole scenario in scratch space and
emits the same evidence document.



Five AWS touchpoints — three verified live


    

  • Lambda verified live — attest-verify-pack
re-verifies a posted pack in AWS using the project's pinned verifier, never the
pack's embedded script — data and code come from different parties:
    • 




  

  
$ aws lambda invoke --function-name attest-verify-pack \
    --payload fileb://pack.json out.json
  {"ok": true} — 2 visit records, 43 receipts proven inside the log
  (transcript of the 2026-09-18 run — today's pack carries 9 visit records)
$ # doctor one byte in a signed payload, invoke again
  {"ok": false} — payload hash mismatch (payload was altered)
  the function catches exactly what the local verifiers catch




    

  • KMS verified live — the Ed25519 signing key
is envelope-encrypted under a KMS data key (AES-256-GCM + encryption context); every
unwrap is a CloudTrail-audited Decrypt, verified end-to-end on a real CMK.
    • 

  • S3 verified live — attest anchor
--publish uploads signed checkpoints to a versioned, public-access-blocked
bucket with the file sha256 stamped into object metadata — a checkpoint outside
the deployment's control.
    • 

  • Bedrock Converse labeled fallback —
multimodal: each camera snapshot's bytes are re-verified against their
signed SHA-256 at summarization time and only digest-verified frames go to
Nova Lite as image blocks — the prompt treats text-in-the-image as untrusted
and forbids identity inference. The account's on-demand quota is zero, so every
model call authenticates then throttles — and the record labels the
deterministic fallback as its true source instead of pretending.
    • 

  • Strands agent labeled fallback — the weekly
triage brief reads the ledger through real read-only tools; same quota wall, same
honest labeling (strands-agent vs deterministic + reason).
    • 



The fallback path is itself verified against real AWS failure modes —
expired aws login creds surface as LoginRefreshRequired,
quota as ThrottlingException — which is the designed behavior, not a
mock. An honest FAIL on the label beats a fake PASS.

What verification proves — and what it doesn't




A green verdict means


    

  • Every signature is valid under the pinned issuer key.
    • 

  • No record was added, dropped, or altered since signing.
    • 

  • The receipts are committed in the signed transparency-log head the pack carries.
    • 

  • Redacted media was withheld by policy — its signed digests still match the manifest.
    • 



It does not mean


    

  • That the worker physically attended — camera activity is an observation, not attendance.
    • 

  • That absence of events means absence — silence isn't absence.
    • 

  • That the issuer is honest — a signature proves integrity under a key, not truth.
That's why witness cosigning and the live-log cross-check exist.
    • 






What's inside the sample pack


The bundled sample-pack.zip is a real export from the
simulated Alvarez residence deployment — media withheld by redaction policy,
signed SHA-256 digests preserved. It exercises the hard parts, not just the happy path:


    

  • a signing-key rotation mid-history: the pack names both issuer keys —
  the rotated-away key, its signed adoption, and the revocation receipt that retires it;
    • 

  • receipts on both sides of the pivot, verified under different keys;
    • 

  • a suspect-window annotation — the window signed while the old key was
  compromised inherits doubt, explicitly;
    • 

  • verifier_manifest.json pinning the verifier tooling's own hashes —
  the pack defends against a hostile verifier shipped inside itself;
    • 

  • log_checkpoint.json: a signed RFC 6962-style Merkle tree head with
  inclusion proofs — the receipts are committed in a transparency log, and the verifier's
  cross-check can prove the deployment still serves the same head to everyone;
    • 

  • a witness cosignature anchored in the witness's own
  independent published log — the attestation is publicly committed the moment
  it's served;
    • 

  • and outside the zip, one click over: divergence-checkpoint.json
  — signed monitor evidence of a caught log fork (the split-demo
  scenario), verified by the same page.
    • 



The embedded index.html is a record browser, not a file listing:
worker, household, and coordinator voices rendered separately from the signed
payload (two conflicting household accounts are flagged, never merged),
per-visit signed anchors and deep links, a plain-language tab that prints clean,
viewer-local times with the signed UTC literal on hover, and a six-element
Cures Act crosswalk — the federal EVV (electronic visit verification)
statute mapped to evidence fields,
labeled as corroboration, never as submission.



Pin the issuer out-of-band. The pack's self-declared key
says who signed it; a judge who doesn't trust the site shouldn't take its word
for the key either. Three ways to pin:


    

  • Fetch the signed issuer document over an independent transport —
  attest-issuer.json
  (the same document a live deployment serves at its origin well-known) — then
  attest verify pack.zip --issuer-url attest-issuer.json. The pack
  and its trust anchor arrive by different surfaces; that's the point.
    • 

  • One click: verify the sample pack
  pinned to the issuer document this site serves — the browser fetches both
  and checks the pin itself.
    • 

  • And the checker defends itself: every pack's signed
  verifier_manifest.json byte-pins verify.html —
  the page hashes its own bytes →
  PINNED/PIN MISMATCH; and
  site-manifest.json pins all
  130 artifacts this site ships → SITE VERIFIED/SITE
  TAMPER, drifted files named.
    •
